# Biomedical conventions: numbers, units, names and people

Use when drafting or checking how results are expressed, how things are named, and how people are described. These are defaults drawn from the ICMJE Recommendations, the AMA Manual of Style (11th edition), the SAGER guidelines, the 2021 JAMA guidance on reporting race and ethnicity, and the nomenclature authorities named below. They were written from those standards without a fresh check of live sources, so confirm any point that matters against the current edition. The target journal's instructions win wherever they differ.

Review conventions by reading, with the journal's instructions open. When you do not know which convention the venue uses, list the deviation for the author instead of normalizing the whole manuscript. Identifiers, gene symbols, catalogue numbers and quoted text are protected fields: flag a suspected error, never restyle it.

## Expressing an effect

The wording of an effect is a scientific choice, and the same estimate can read as trivial or as important depending on how it is written. Choose the clearest expression that is true to the measure.

- **Percent change versus "times".** For a ratio below about 2, the percent change usually reads more naturally and more precisely than a multiple: a rate ratio of 1.52 is "52% higher", which is clearer than "1.5 times" and stronger than the vague "1.5 to 1.6 times". For large ratios a multiple is clearer ("nearly fivefold, from 2.1 to 9.8 per 1000"). Give the underlying values alongside either form when space allows.
- **Use the noun the measure supports.** A rate ratio describes rates, a hazard ratio hazards, an odds ratio odds. An odds ratio of 1.52 means 52% higher odds. For an absolute risk comparison, use observed risks or appropriately estimated probabilities for an explicit population and time horizon. Changing the noun in a regression result does not calculate a risk. [Cochrane Handbook, chapter 15](https://training.cochrane.org/handbook/current/chapter-15)
- **Be exact, not approximately exact.** A range such as "1.5 to 1.6 times" for two related estimates reads as imprecision. Report each estimate with its interval, or the one that matters.
- **Pair relative with absolute.** For clinical outcomes give the absolute risks or the difference alongside a ratio when the data allow. A number needed to treat needs its time horizon and comes from the absolute difference; it is a new calculation, not a rewording.
- **Percent versus percentage points.** A change from 12% to 18% is 6 percentage points, or a 50% relative increase. Say which.
- **Say which direction and against what.** Name the reference group and the unit of a continuous exposure ("per 10 mm Hg", "per 1-SD increase").
- **Retire the phrases that stand in for a result:** "trend toward significance", "marginally significant", "highly significant", "borderline". Report the estimate and interval and let them speak.

## Numbers and statistics

- Report estimates with their intervals when available. For observed percentages, identify the numerator and eligible denominator. For weighted, standardized or time-to-event estimates, identify the estimation method and distinguish the underlying sample size from the estimated percentage. Precision should match the sample: with fewer than about 100 observations, whole-number percentages are usually enough.
- Report a P value as the number the output gives ("P = .03") in preference to a threshold or "NS", to the precision the analysis supports and the journal requests. Many journals use "P < .001" as the smallest reported value. Preserve a reported inequality when only a bound is known. A display rounded to zero needs the underlying output or a supported bound; never write "P = .000" or supply digits the output lacks. Capitalization, italics and the leading zero follow the journal (AMA style omits the leading zero; many journals keep it).
- Use "to" in intervals that include negative values ("−4.3 to 0.9") so a hyphen is not mistaken for a minus sign.
- State whether a spread is an SD, SE, IQR or range; a bare "±" is ambiguous. Give an IQR as its two quartiles. Choose mean (SD) or median (IQR) by the distribution, and use the same choice for the same variable throughout.
- Keep the number of decimals consistent within a measure and no finer than the measurement.
- Recast a sentence so that it does not open with a numeral.

## Units and measurement

- Use SI or conventional units as the journal requires; if both are needed, give the conversion once.
- Doses carry amount, unit, route and frequency. Centrifugation is reported in × g, concentrations in mol/L or a defined unit, temperatures in °C.
- Ages and durations carry units ("aged 65 years or older"); dates are unambiguous.

## Names

- **Drugs:** nonproprietary (generic) names in lower case. Give a brand name only when the specific product matters.
- **Genes and proteins:** approved symbols (HGNC for human genes). Human gene symbols are italic capitals (*TP53*) and the protein is roman (TP53 or p53); mouse genes are italic with an initial capital (*Trp53*). Describe variants in HGVS nomenclature against a named reference sequence. Check tables for symbols that spreadsheet software has converted to dates.
- **Organisms:** italic binomial, genus capitalized, genus abbreviated after first use.
- **Eponyms:** nonpossessive in AMA style (Parkinson disease, Down syndrome); other journals differ.
- **Reagents, devices, cell lines, software:** name, version or catalogue identifier, and manufacturer or RRID as supplied. Never supply one from memory.
- **Trials and cohorts:** registry identifier where one exists; acronyms expanded at first use.
- **Abbreviations:** define at first use in the abstract and again in the main text. Do not abbreviate a term used only a few times, and do not coin abbreviations for the paper. Standard units need no definition.
- **Measured outcomes keep their measured names.** A framing term may explain a concept in the Introduction or Discussion ("time-sensitive conditions"), while the outcome is still called what was measured ("out-of-hospital cardiac arrest").

## People

- "Participants" or "patients" in preference to "subjects". Put the person first or use the term the community prefers ("patients with diabetes", not "diabetics"). Avoid "suffering from", "victim", "failed treatment" and "noncompliant"; prefer "did not respond to" and "adherence".
- **Sex and gender** are different constructs. Report which was measured and how, and do not interchange the terms (SAGER).
- **Race and ethnicity:** say how the categories were determined and by whom, and why they were collected. Use specific categories, capitalize them, avoid a collapsed "other" where the data allow, and never treat race as a proxy for genetics or biology. Ancestry is not race.
- **Age:** give ranges; "older adults", not "the elderly".
- **Case descriptions:** include only the detail the clinical point requires, and check for indirect identifiers.

## Tense and person

Methods and Results are in the past tense; established knowledge is in the present. First-person plural ("we") is accepted by most journals and usually clearer than a passive detour; keep the choice consistent. Name the design in the title or abstract where the reporting guideline asks for it.
