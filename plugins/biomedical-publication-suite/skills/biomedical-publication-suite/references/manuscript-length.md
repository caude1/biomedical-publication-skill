# Manuscript length and section balance

Use when planning, drafting or substantially revising a complete manuscript, adapting to a journal, or when the user flags short, bloated or unbalanced sections. Length is an editorial decision about the article's scientific job; counting helps check that decision. Do not impose one size on every paper or pad a passage to satisfy a local heuristic.

## Establish the right target

**Target journal known:** inspect its current official instructions for the exact article type. Record the maximum and counting rules: main text, abstract, title/statement pages, headings, tables/captions, references and supplements may be treated differently. Distinguish a maximum from a recommended range and verify separate abstract/display limits. Treat a maximum as a ceiling. Use the space needed to make the argument complete, expose the methods that matter, and interpret the finding. A generous ceiling can leave considerable room unused. An explicit author target or a venue's recommended range is a separate constraint.

**No journal target:** identify article type, design, scientific complexity, audience and depth of contribution. Inspect a small set of accessible, recent comparable papers with similar study type and scope; compare both total main-text length and section proportions. A style comparator guides architecture, not factual claims. Do not infer body length from a PDF page count or a truncated abstract. Exclude references, captions and supplementary text consistently when comparing. Use available XML/text or a light word count when helpful; this is editorial reconnaissance, not a new systematic review.

If comparators are unavailable, choose a reasonable provisional budget using article type and scientific content, then explain the assumption only if material to the handoff. For a conventional full original clinical/epidemiologic paper, roughly 3,000–4,000 main-text words is a useful starting estimate, not a minimum, maximum or claimed journal standard. Move outside it when the actual design, scope or user's constraints justify that choice. Do not apply it to letters, short reports, protocols, reviews or substantial methods articles.

## Allocate by function

Before drafting, form a rough total and a few section targets in existing notes or mentally. No separate budget file is required. For a conventional original-research paper, the following is one illustrative allocation—not a template to force onto every study:

| Section | Example within a 3,500-word main text | What earns the space |
|---|---:|---|
| Introduction | 450 | Clinical/scientific problem, specific gap, objective |
| Methods | 900 | Essential design, population, measurement, endpoints and analysis needed to assess/reproduce the main work |
| Results | 850 | Cohort/flow, pivotal findings, important supporting and contrary results without repeating every display cell |
| Discussion, including limitations/conclusion | 1,300 | Interpretation of the main contribution, compatible literature, explanation/alternatives, implications and proportionate limitations |

The example sums to 3,500 words and excludes abstract, references, display captions and supplements. It is an editorial planning example, not a researched venue requirement. Methods-heavy designs may need more methods; a focused report may need less of every section. A rich clinical paper usually needs more than three short Discussion paragraphs to interpret ten paragraphs of Methods and several important results, but paragraph counts alone do not determine balance.

Allocate by reasoning depth, not number of variables or scripts. Results need not repeat table contents. Discussion should develop the scientific meaning rather than pad itself with result recap or generic future work. A brief but rigorous Introduction is often better than an expansive general background. Preserve enough main-text methods to evaluate validity; transfer only permitted supporting technical detail to a supplement and retain pointers.

## Adapt the article type

- **Brief report/research letter:** one focused question, compact methods/results, selective interpretation; use verified venue/comparator length. Do not deliver a compressed full paper that still tries to cover every branch.
- **Systematic/meta-analytic review:** space follows the question, review methods, evidence structure and synthesis; diagrams/tables can carry descriptive study details. Do not compress the methods required for reproducible review conduct or give each included paper equal prose.
- **Narrative/scoping review:** organization and length follow conceptual scope/mapping purpose. Avoid an unbounded paper-by-paper catalogue.
- **Protocol/methods paper:** planned design or reproducible method can appropriately dominate; no Results/Discussion budget for nonexistent findings.
- **Case report/qualitative/implementation paper:** use the relevant genre and reporting needs; a clinically necessary narrative or analytic theme structure can replace conventional proportions.

## Rebalance before delivery

Read an uninterrupted draft, then inspect section/total counts with the actual counting exclusions. A simple word count is useful measurement, not an automated editorial score. Check whether the main question and contribution receive enough space and whether the scientific progression remains coherent.

If **too short**, identify missing intellectual work: the specific literature gap, why the final methods answer it, interpretation of the pivotal finding, a meaningful comparison with prior evidence, mechanisms/alternatives appropriately labeled, or concrete implications. Add supported reasoning and verified literature where needed; do not repeat facts or inflate sentences.

If **too long**, reorganize before trimming. Removing words and clauses in place leaves choppy sentences and disconnected ideas, and the argument degrades faster than the count falls. Find the paragraphs that overlap or that serve a point the paper no longer makes, and rewrite them as fewer paragraphs with one job each; a rebuilt paragraph is usually shorter and reads better than a whittled one. Then take the cheap cuts that cost nothing (a repeated definition, a restated result, a sentence of signposting). Protect the sentences that help the reader follow the reasoning, such as a clause saying what a check rules out, even when they are easy to delete. Cut duplicated arguments, unnecessary background, Results restated in Discussion, workflow history, extended peripheral analyses, overexplained captions and routine technical detail better placed in a supplement. Compress secondary results without hiding evidence that changes the main conclusion.

If **unbalanced**, ask whether each section performs its job. Many Methods paragraphs with only a few undeveloped Discussion paragraphs often signals insufficient interpretation. Expand the argument where warranted and consolidate technical detail; do not simply lengthen Discussion to match another section numerically.

Meet the agreed target or verified venue rules and preserve scientific completeness. If a requested target conflicts with adequate reporting, explain the specific tradeoff; do not claim that a short draft is a completed full manuscript merely because its headings are present. Report actual counts on request or during journal adaptation, along with what was included, rather than guessed counts.
