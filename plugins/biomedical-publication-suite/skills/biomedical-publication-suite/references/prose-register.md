# Prose register review for scientific text

Use this after the science, the claims and the structure are settled, before the [cold editorial read](manuscript-voice.md). It edits register only: how a sentence carries its claim, never what the claim is. [Scientific editing](scientific-humanization.md) still governs edit intensity, authority and the meaning ledger.

Do this review by reading, not with a script. Whether a shape is a tic depends on the section, the claim, the field's conventions and the author's voice, and pattern matching cannot judge any of those. A phrase list finds candidates and misses the next paraphrase; a clean scan proves nothing. The reviewer reads every sentence and decides.

## The test

For each sentence ask: *does this sentence state a result, a method or an inference, or does it perform the arrival of one?* In a manuscript the estimate, the comparison and the design-matched verb carry the claim. A sentence that makes the reader feel a finding land, instead of telling them the finding, is staging. The fix is always the same: put the real subject in the subject slot, state the thing with its number, and stop.

The opposite failure matters as much. A confident rewrite tends to land in flat certainty, where an adverb or an absolute stands in for the evidence ("clearly demonstrates", "no evidence of"). Confidence in scientific prose comes from the estimate beside the claim, never from the adverb in front of it.

## The register to aim for

Scientific prose fails in three directions, and fixing one often produces another.

- **Performed.** Pithy lines, aphorisms, a colon that withholds the payload for a beat ("The explanation is simple: …"), a narrator's summary of the evidence ("Two results tell the same story"), a metaphor doing the work of a mechanism ("a canary for the health system"), the confidence of a keynote. A scientific paper or talk is not a TED talk.
- **Loose.** Conversational or blunt phrasing a journal would not print: "the numbers don't lie", "patients fall through the cracks", "the clinic was flying blind". Informality usually hides imprecision; the formal version names the thing.
- **Convoluted.** A sentence that carries a finding, two qualifiers and two contrasts, so the reader has to untangle which clause modifies what. This is the usual result of compressing a staged sentence instead of rebuilding it.

The target is the sentence a careful clinician-scientist would write and a copy editor would leave alone: one main idea, the comparison stated outright, the number in it, formal without being ornate. When a sentence is flagged as any of the three, rebuild it from what it has to say; do not trade one failure for another.

## What to look for, by where it concentrates

Read the families that fit the artifact first. The specimens are illustrations; the reviewer's judgment decides each instance.

**Abstract conclusion, Discussion opener, paragraph closers, headings** (staging concentrates here in model-written drafts):

- *Negation-first reveal.* "Patients contacted by portal did not simply book less often; they booked later." State the positive finding with its estimate. Earned when the reader genuinely holds the rejected reading, for example a cited prior explanation: "An earlier report attributed missed visits to travel distance; in this cohort, adding travel time to the model changed the odds ratio from 1.42 to 1.35."
- *Significance designation.* "This is the key finding", "the analysis that answers the central question". Name the comparison that makes it important, or let its position in the paper say so.
- *Aphoristic closer.* A paragraph ending on a maxim ("A booking, in other words, is not a visit."). End on the fact or the supported implication. A short closer that states a fact is fine.
- *Verdict or coy heading.* "The gap that survives adjustment" as a Results subheading. Use a plain label unless the venue uses declarative headings. IMRaD headings, structured-abstract labels and journal-required headings (Data Availability, Funding) are never tics, whatever their case.
- *Rhetorical question.* "Is a missed first visit inevitable?" in a Discussion. State the answer. A labelled research question (Key Points "Question", a stated hypothesis) is required content.
- *Story agency.* "The data tell a compelling story", "a clear picture emerges". Report what was measured. Display and evidence verbs with inanimate subjects are conventional ("Table 2 shows", "Figure 3 presents", "these findings suggest"); verbs of judgment belong to people ("this analysis cannot exclude" becomes "we cannot exclude").
- *Content-free evaluation.* "This is a noteworthy finding." with nothing after it. Keep it only if a "because" with real content follows.
- *Colon reveal and narrator summary.* "The pattern was consistent: deaths rose only where…", "Two results stand out." State the result as a sentence with its subject and verb. A colon before a list or a definition is ordinary punctuation.
- *Retroactive significance and false concession.* "Despite these limitations, our findings provide important insights", "While X is impressive, Y remains a challenge". Keep both claims if both are real; do not let a formula pick a side for the author.
- *Announce, then enumerate.* "Two intervals matter after referral: …", "Two findings suggest both steps matter." The sentence counts what is coming instead of saying it. Write the first finding as a sentence and let the second follow from it.
- *Tagline sentence.* A short declarative that labels instead of stating: "Booking is the weak step.", "Staffing is the likely link.", "Three findings moved together." Say what was measured and how the findings relate, with the numbers that show it.
- *Coined term or recurring metaphor.* A name the paper invents for its own concept ("the two clocks") or an image used as a mechanism ("along this path"). Use the conventional term, which may itself be figurative ("tracer condition" is established in health services research); a figurative phrase can explain a concept once, in the Discussion, if it earns its place.

**Introduction** (encyclopedic defaults):

- Inflated importance: "plays a crucial role", "sheds light on", "a growing body of evidence", "paradigm shift", "underscores the critical need". Replace with the specific fact and its citation, or cut. Never replace it with a fact you supply.
- Vague attribution: "studies have consistently shown", "experts agree", with no citation beside it.
- Copula avoidance ("serves as", "stands as", "represents a"), participle tails asserting significance (", highlighting the need for…"), forced lists of three abstract nouns, false ranges ("from bench to bedside, from cells to populations").
- Elegant variation: the same cohort called "participants", "the sample", "subjects" and "the population" in one section. Technical repetition is correct.
- Invented concept labels ("the calibration paradox", "the access trap") and invented contrast mirrors ("false precision rather than genuine accuracy").

**Methods and Results** (compression and flat certainty):

- *Noun stacks.* "clinic staffing level follow-up visit attendance association". Unpack with prepositions and verbs. The fix makes the sentence longer, and that is correct: shorter is not plainer. An established term, or a term expanded once before its abbreviation, is fine.
- *Dropped "that", distant verbs, chained ", and" clauses.* A subject waiting behind a covariate list for its verb; ", and this…" with no clear referent; stacked comparison conditions in one sentence. Name the relation (because, whereas, although) and separate conditions.
- *Flat-certainty adverbs and compounds.* "clearly demonstrate", "highly significant", "rigorously validated", "well-powered", "fully adjusted". Earned when the evidence sits beside it: the power calculation, the adjustment set, the validation statistic.
- *Exhaustive negation.* "There was no evidence of an association." Give the estimate and interval, or the test and its result. "No participant had grade 4 neutropenia" is a count and stays. See [statistical reporting](statistical-reporting.md) on nonsignificance.
- *Unresolved comparisons and scope.* "The association was just as strong" (as what?), "38% of older patients" (of all older patients, or of those treated?), a modifier that seems to apply to one group when it applies to all, a sentence in which the reader cannot tell prior studies from the present one. Name the comparator, the denominator and whose result it is.
- *Undefined working terms.* A phrase the argument leans on ("timely treatment", "guideline-concordant care") that is never pinned to what was measured.
- *Ornamental adverbs.* "markedly", "remarkably", "significantly" without a test. Delete one and see whether information is lost. "approximately", "consistently" and "differed significantly (P = .003)" carry information.
- *One construction on repeat.* "To test whether…, we…" three times in a paragraph, two consecutive sentences opening with "Because", ", so" as the default joint between clauses. Each is fine once; vary the construction when it recurs.
- *Bullet points in paragraph form.* Sentences that each state one fact, in an order that could be shuffled, with no relation named between them. Rebuild the paragraph around how the facts connect.

**Limitations:** the listicle in disguise ("The first limitation… The second limitation… The third…"), boilerplate threats with no named direction, and a closing "Despite these limitations" pivot.

**Letters and responses:** formula praise on every reviewer comment ("We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment"); one thanks per letter is courtesy. Announced sincerity in cover letters ("We are genuinely excited"). Chatbot residue anywhere ("I hope this helps", "Here is the revised version").

**Leaks in any artifact:** unfilled placeholders (`[INSERT CITATION]`, `2025-XX-XX`), chat-tool citation tokens (`citeturn0search0`, `contentReference[oaicite:0]`), assistant referrer parameters in URLs (`utm_source=chatgpt.com`), and reasoning-chain text. These are always defects.

## Leave these alone

These are field conventions or the author's voice, not tics:

- Discourse markers with a job: "Notably,", "Importantly,", "Interestingly,", "In addition,", "Moreover,", "Furthermore,", "For example,", "As expected,", "Consistent with this,", "Specifically,", "Thus,", "In agreement with previous reports". Judge the sentence they open, not the marker. Phrases that state how ideas relate help a reader, and adding one where the relation is only implicit is often the right edit. When a transition does go, restore the logical link (a connective, an echoed noun, a relation-naming conjunction); never leave two sentences sitting side by side with no link.
- Literal terms: robust standard errors, pivotal trial, mutational landscape, unmeasured confounder, key secondary end point, leverage points.
- Real lists of three: outcomes, covariates, sites, groups.
- Short declarative sentences in Methods and Results, passive voice where the procedure carries focus, nominal headings, and title case where the house style uses it.
- Qualifiers that define the claim: "among patients aged 65 and older", "in the complete-case analysis", "registered", "at least 25 of 77".
- One hedge that names its condition. Design-matched verbs ("was associated with") are accuracy, not hedging.
- En dashes in ranges and compound names; em dashes at the rate the author's own sample or the journal style uses them.
- Uneven depth, a stated surprise ("We did not expect the association to persist after adjustment"), specific hard-to-invent detail, and the author's approved opinions. Do not weaken "We expect X to play a central role" into "X may potentially".

One instance of a shape is rarely a pattern. Look for clusters and for reuse: a construction used three times in one Discussion is a habit; once is a choice.

## Rules that protect the science

1. **Read the whole artifact before editing.** A sentence built for shape is disproportionately likely to be wrong. A Discussion that names "the key finding" may name a secondary endpoint; the abstract may contradict Table 2. Note every contradiction and report it to the author separately. Never silently fix one; only the author knows which version is true.
2. **Find the point first.** Write in one sentence of your own what the paper wants the reader to leave knowing. If you cannot, or if paragraphs can be reshuffled without loss, or if half the text could be cut with nothing missing, the problem is the argument, not the sentences: go to [narrative framing](narrative-frameworks.md) and rebuild from the point rather than repairing sentence by sentence.
3. **Never invent specifics.** The fix for a vague sentence is a specific one, which is exactly how a register pass fabricates. A specific must come from the study outputs, a retrieved source or the author. Prefer cutting or narrowing a vague sentence to what the source supports. Where the passage cannot do its job without a number or citation you do not have, write `[DATA NEEDED: what is missing]` and list it for the author. Do not scatter markers for detail the original never contained (a time window, a second result the draft only alludes to); mention such a gap once, in a short note, and return clean text. Keep anything you recall but have not verified in a separate "unverified candidates" list, never in the text.
4. **Constructions carry claims.** Superlatives ("the largest reduction"), rankings, scope words ("all", "only", "each"), simultaneity ("concurrently"), negations and the condition attached to a hedge all live inside phrasing a register pass cuts. Before and after, account for each one claim by claim, alongside the numbers and citations in the meaning ledger. Rewriting "the decline was largest among patients over 80" as "age drives the decline" invents a causal finding.
5. **Do not inject.** A rewrite must not add manufactured stakes, invented experience, new em dashes, staccato fragments or new hedges. The rewrite reads more naturally because it says the thing directly, not because it acquired new rhetoric.
6. **Overcorrection is worse than the tics.** Flat is not hedged: target plain and sure, never plain and timid, and keep uncertainty that changes interpretation. Do not enforce uniform sentence length, strip first-person judgment the venue allows, or delete a field-standard metaphor.
7. **The author's writing wins.** Given a sample of the author's prose or their published papers, match its habits over every rule here.
8. **Never optimize for detectors.** Nothing in this review says anything about who or what wrote the text.

## How to run the review

1. Read the whole artifact once without editing; note contradictions and the one-sentence point.
2. Read the slots where staging concentrates: every heading, the title, the abstract conclusion, the first sentence of the Discussion, the last sentence of each Discussion paragraph, any one-sentence paragraph, and figure titles. For a near-final artifact, use [multi-round cold reads](cold-reader-rounds.md).
3. Sentence pass, starting with the families above that fit each section.
4. **Newcomer read** when the text was revised right after the analysis or the project changed (an audit, a reanalysis, a revision round). Look for phrases that lean on a version the reader never saw: "the corrected cohort", "the revised exposure definition", "now", "no longer", "the updated model", "as before", a Methods paragraph that explains how a problem was fixed instead of what the procedure is, or one section still describing the old analysis. The person who made the revision cannot be this reader, so give the current text, without the revision history, to a fresh subagent (the newcomer assignment in [multi-round cold reads](cold-reader-rounds.md)). Weigh each reported problem against the evidence and revise the passages that depend on knowledge the reader lacks.
5. Account for the claim-carrying constructions (rule 4) and the meaning ledger.
6. Report factual problems separately from the edited text, with their locators.

For a full catalogue of mechanisms with before-and-after pairs, the declauding skill's `references/register.md` and `references/exceptions.md` are worth reading when they are installed. Read them as background; this guide decides what counts as earned in scientific prose.
