# Clarity for a reader with no context

The reader of a manuscript has never seen the project. They do not know which analyses were tried, which problems were found along the way, what vocabulary the team adopted, or why a choice that now feels obvious to its authors was made. Aim to bring the effort this reader spends on understanding close to zero. At no point should they have to stop and ask what a sentence means, why the authors did something, or what a phrase refers to. A clinical reader of a methods-heavy paper (a clustering or latent class model, a machine learning classifier, a quasi-experimental design) needs this most, because the method is unfamiliar and every unexplained step costs attention.

Neither the author nor the agent who drafted the text can test this alone. Knowing the project fills every gap, so a sentence can read clearly to its writers and still lose a newcomer. For near-final work, use the [newcomer and first-time clinical readers](cold-reader-rounds.md). Until then, read each passage as someone who has only the page.

## Spend the reader's attention on what earns it

What the team worried about during the analysis is rarely what a reader will think about. A data-recording problem that took a week to resolve, a check run to settle an internal doubt, or an alternative explanation the team ruled out can each feel central to the people who did the work. The argument about them belongs in the main text only if a reader would raise the concern unprompted. A defense against a concern the reader never had plants that concern and makes the paper sound unsure of itself. This test removes argument, not procedure. A step that changed the data or the analysis (an exclusion, a recoding, a correction), or an assumption the method needs, is stated in the Methods as what was done, whether or not a reader would ask, and anything a reporting guideline or the journal requires stays. What goes is the story of finding the problem and the defense of the fix. Use the space for the features that make the result believable to an outside reader, such as comparison outcomes that behaved as predicted, a gradient with the extent of exposure, agreement between independent sources, or a design that rules out the most obvious alternative. Allocate space by the ranking in [narrative framing](narrative-frameworks.md), which puts the primary result, harms, and contrary evidence first, and within it spend the explanatory effort on what a newcomer needs. Report comparison outcomes that did not behave as predicted as prominently as those that did.

The same test decides which robustness and sensitivity analyses appear in the main text; [statistical reporting](statistical-reporting.md) gives the rule and its floor. An analysis earns main-text space when a plausible alternative could change the conclusion and the analysis addresses it, or when it carries part of the interpretation, such as an analysis limited to patients who completed follow-up when dropout could explain a difference. To a clinical reader, a count of alternative specifications, a median across hundreds of them, or a sequence of single-variable swaps can read as a search for significance, even when it is the opposite. Keep such work out of the abstract and usually out of the main text; where it must appear, one sentence with a supplement pointer is enough. An analysis that weakens or contradicts a claim is different. It changes the claim in the main text and is never held back to protect the story.

## Give every choice that could look arbitrary its reason

A reader who meets an unexplained number or rule tends to assume it was chosen to produce the result. Each threshold, cutoff, study period, count of included units, comparison outcome, and model term that could look arbitrary carries its reason where it first appears. Examples include the origin of a number ("a cutoff of 3.84, the χ² critical value with 1 degree of freedom at P = .05"; "the 30 clinics that accept direct referrals", not just "30 clinics"), the reason for a period ("from 2015, when the referral system began recording the contact channel"), and the reason each comparison outcome is in the list, so that none looks added at random. When the literature already supports a choice, a citation can carry the justification in place of an argument. A citation found after the choice was made is worded as precedent ("a distance used in earlier studies"), not as the reason the choice was made.

State each reason as design logic that holds independently of the results. Take it from the study record or the authors, and if the record gives a narrower reason, state that reason rather than widening it. Never invent a rationale the record does not support, and never present a choice as planned in advance unless a dated protocol shows it. A choice that depended on the results is described as it was made, and flagged for the authors. Describe a rule by the principle that set it, not by what it preserved. "The strictest missingness threshold that kept the staffing measure" sounds like a threshold tuned to keep one variable. "The strictest threshold at which every clinic still had a measure of staffing, attendance, and travel time" states the principle. Replace a vague quantifier in Methods ("most visits", "a few clinics") with the rule that decides membership, or include all cases. If a choice cannot be justified this way, the analysis may need to change, which better wording cannot repair.

Give the reason in a clause or a sentence, not a paragraph. Over-explaining is the failure on the other side. A Methods section that narrates every consideration buries the procedure and loses the reader in a different way. Answer the question the reader would ask with the shortest clause that removes it.

## Sentences the reader can follow on one pass

- **Name the comparator.** "Just as much", "as strong", "not smaller", and "lower" each need their object ("lower than among patients contacted by telephone"). Give the direction of a difference ("1.0 point higher in the portal group"). A negated comparison such as "the association was not smaller" is usually clearer stated positively.
- **Make scope and denominators unambiguous.** In "Of the clinics that lost a physician, 65% were rural, which receive 30% of referrals, and their median wait doubled", the reader cannot tell whether only rural waits doubled or whose 30% is meant. Give each fact its own clause, subject, and denominator.
- **Say whose study it is.** At each mention, make clear whether a finding comes from prior work or from this study. For another analysis of the same cohort or registry, write "a separate study of the [cohort name]", and for the published work of the same authors, say so ("We previously reported"). Phrases such as "in this cohort", "in the present cohort", or "an earlier analysis of this cohort" read as the current analysis or as an earlier attempt by the authors.
- **Put the subject and its verb early.** A sentence that opens with a phrase the reader cannot attach until the end ("appearing in follow-up visits but not in first visits...") makes them hold that phrase in memory. Name the subject more often than seems necessary, and prefer a referring noun ("This delay", "These visits") to a bare "It" or "This".
- **Mind what the reader anchors on.** The first term in a sentence, and the first sentence of a paragraph, set the default reading of everything after them. A clause that sounds like a negative result ("whereas attendance did not change among patients who booked") reads as a contradiction if the reader has not yet learned why it was expected. Establish the expectation first, or move the clause. Likewise, a qualifier in the first mention of a term ("losing evening hours" where the paper means losing the whole clinic) becomes the default meaning.
- **Group related ideas for the reader.** A short phrase that ties a second finding to the first ("after these staffing changes", "accordingly") shows a first-time reader that two facts belong to one argument.
- **Avoid words with a technical meaning nearby.** "Selected" near a discussion of selection bias, "significant" without a test, "random", "normal", and "power" are read in their technical sense. Choose another word or make the sense explicit.
- **Replace vague nouns.** "Aspects", "factors", "impact", and catch-all labels such as "chronic disease" leave the reader to guess. Name what was measured.
- **Describe populations and procedures in plain terms.** "Of the 310 referrals with the data needed to apply the rule" is clearer than "Of the 310 referrals whose recorded answers decided the rule (the other 40 lacked an answer it needed)". A parenthesis the reader has to decode costs more than the precision it adds.
- **Lead with the measures, not a taxonomy.** A Methods paragraph that defines a hierarchy of abstract terms (domains, constructs, indicators, alternatives) before saying what was measured loses the clinical reader. Say what was measured and why, then name the abstraction only if later text uses it. A coined term that appears once ("the other variables, termed alternatives") can usually go.
- **Label every statistic.** Say what each P value tests (difference, trend, interaction), give a unit for every laboratory or physical measure, and keep the definition of a dichotomized outcome visible wherever it could be misread. Report every level of a graded comparison with its interval, not only the significant ones. Report effects on a scale the reader can weigh; see [conventions](biomedical-conventions.md).
- **Calibrate in both directions.** Use the strongest wording the evidence supports. When two groups are nearly identical on a descriptive measure the argument depends on, such as a baseline characteristic, "similar" understates the evidence. Write "nearly identical" and give the numbers. For an outcome or an effect, the estimate and its interval decide, and a small difference is not equivalence. "Significantly" is useful where a test supports it. Overclaiming fails as well. Match the claim to what was tested (an estimate for all clinics does not show that the effect reached the satellite clinics), and cite or soften an a priori statement that is only mostly true ("rarely affected by scheduling", not "unaffected by scheduling").

## Paragraphs that guide the reader

- **Each sentence builds on the one before.** The reader should be able to say why each sentence follows the previous one. A paragraph built as a list of facts, one per sentence, reads like bullet points converted into prose. Connect them through their actual relations. Manuscripts use paragraph prose, not bullet points.
- **Give context before the reason that depends on it.** A Methods paragraph that justifies its unit of analysis before saying where the data came from asks the reader to accept a rationale for something they have not yet met.
- **Keep related ideas together.** A rule belongs with what it means for patients, a result with its interpretation, and a definition with its first use. A sentence that seems out of place usually belongs in another paragraph. A paragraph of one or two sentences usually belongs inside a neighbor or needs to become a full paragraph. Discuss each topic in one place.
- **Let transitions announce the role of the next sentence.** The reader should know, before reading it, whether a sentence adds a prediction, a contrast, an example, or a consequence ("Specifically,", "By contrast,", "For example,", "As a result,"). A join that does not tell them reads as choppy.
- **Welcome connectives, and vary them.** Phrases that state how ideas relate ("For example,", "As expected,", "Consistent with this,", "By contrast,") help the reader, and they can be added where a relation is only implicit. Prefer the one that names the specific relation; an additive one ("Moreover,", "Furthermore,", "In addition,") suits a parallel point but should not pad. Vary them across the paper. The same construction opening sentence after sentence (", so", "Because", "To test whether") becomes its own tic.
- **Do not repeat a distinctive word or opening nearby.** Two paragraphs that open alike, or a distinctive word ("gap") or stock phrase ("these findings") in neighboring sentences, read as unedited. Technical terms and referring nouns repeat as needed.
- **Thread a framework through the paper.** A framework the paper relies on is introduced as a reason for the design, organizes the Methods and the tables where it fits, and returns in the Discussion. Mentioned in a few isolated sentences, it reads as tacked on.
- **Keep the explanatory frame when rewriting.** When a paragraph is rebuilt for clarity, keep the reasoning that explains why the findings make sense within the argument. A clearer paragraph that has lost that explanation is only a list of results.

## Methods-heavy papers for clinical readers

- Name the method in plain words with what it does for this question, in one clause ("a random forest, which combines many decision trees fitted to resamples of the data"), and give the reason, from the record, for choosing it over the obvious alternative.
- Name the software actually used, with its version. A standard method is best fitted with an established, cited package; when the authors used their own code, recommend a refit with the package to them, outside the text. If their code stays, the paper says so with the reason, and agreement with an established package takes a clause or a supplement sentence ([statistical reporting](statistical-reporting.md)).
- Prefer standard metrics a clinician can interpret (agreement as κ, calibration as observed against predicted risk) to specialist diagnostics, which can go in the supplement.
- When a classification or model is meant for use, show in the main text how a clinician would apply it (a short rule or tree) with its agreement with the original classification or, for a prediction rule, its discrimination and calibration, and say whether it was evaluated only in the patients it was derived from. Put exact weights or equations in the supplement, and prefer the simpler rule when its cost is small and stated.
- Leave out file names, internal notes, and sentences about the document itself ("We capitalize the names of the groups"). Fitting details that a reporting guideline asks for, such as the number of random starting values, stay.

## Running the friction read

Read the whole draft once as a newcomer, then section by section, marking each place where a newcomer would stop to ask one of the three questions. Fix the cause rather than the symptom: a sentence that seems to need a definition may need a different term, and a paragraph with an unclear purpose may belong elsewhere. Then check the fixes for overcorrection. Each addition should be the shortest clause that removes the question, and the revised text should be no more hedged than the evidence requires. When a reader flags a procedural statement as unnecessary, have a methods reader confirm the cut.
