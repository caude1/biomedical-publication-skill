# Sections and publication artifacts

Use the user's article type and current journal rules. For a full paper set a total and section budget with [length and balance](manuscript-length.md) before drafting. A generic structured abstract can use Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions, but no house structure is mandatory. [Article types](article-types.md) handles exceptions; [statistical reporting](statistical-reporting.md) governs numbers.

| Artifact | Scientific job and essential checks |
|---|---|
| Title | Identify question/construct/population and design when useful; avoid causal or novelty claims beyond evidence; check abbreviation and length rules |
| Abstract | Reconcile design, setting/population, endpoint, primary estimates/intervals, consequential harms/nulls; a limitation only where the conclusion would otherwise mislead; a take-home close; no results absent from body |
| Introduction | Verified context → specific gap → objective/hypothesis with actual epistemic status; no manufactured novelty or retrofitted prespecification |
| Methods | Reproduce what was done: design/setting/dates, eligibility/flow, measurements/intervention, outcomes, sample rationale, analysis/missingness/multiplicity, software, ethics/registration when documented; retain required rationale/deviations |
| Results | Cohort/flow, outcome-specific denominators, primary findings, harms, secondary/exploratory/sensitivity findings; align every display and avoid inference beyond analysis |
| Discussion | Answer question, compare compatible literature, explain interpretation and alternatives, bounded implications, specific limitations, proportionate conclusion |
| Highlights | Distinct verified take-home points within current venue limits; no new findings or unsupported importance claims |
| Cover letter | Title/type, question/finding, fit to verified scope, supported required declarations; no invented exclusivity, author approval, prior contact, or novelty |
| Plain-language summary | Define needed terms; benefits/harms and uncertainty symmetrically; comparable absolute risks with same denominator/time; no individualized clinical advice from population findings |
| Graphical/structured summary | One question and supported conclusion; explicit design/comparison; label conceptual mechanisms as proposed; source-map quantitative displays |
| Supplement | Reproducible detailed methods/results, data dictionaries, checklists; stable labels and main-text pointers; consistent endpoints and versions |

Methods generally reports completed work in past tense; a protocol reports plans. Preserve exact units, identifiers, equations, concentrations, versions, durations, and quote/citation fields. Never supply conventional ethics or consent wording without evidence.

Find the central scientific contribution before allocating paragraph space. Develop the important/interesting thread coherently; report peripheral findings briefly without equal-weight discussion. A requested Discussion opening should establish and interpret the contribution; it need not include a limitations paragraph, peripheral covariate inventory or future-research ending. Reserve those for their appropriate place in a full Discussion. For substantial edits, reverse-outline paragraph roles and repair claim-to-evidence links. Do not force section restructuring for a light edit. Technical terminology consistency outweighs stylistic variation.

**Conclusion.** Some readers read only the conclusion, so it must stand alone: the population, the main finding with its number, and the implication the evidence supports, ending on that implication. It is a statement of what the paper established, written fresh, not a compressed repeat of the Discussion's first paragraph. One closing sentence that draws the argument together is welcome when it says something; a slogan is not.

**Discussion.** Unless the journal prescribes a structure, the first paragraph orients a reader who skipped straight to it: the design and population in a phrase, the main findings with the few numbers the argument rests on, and what the findings mean. It summarizes; it does not re-list the Results. The paragraphs that follow explain the findings, usually by addressing the obvious alternative explanations with evidence and then the mechanism, and compare them with prior work where the comparison helps the interpretation. They then develop the implications the design supports for the people who can act on them, such as clinicians, health systems, or policy makers, concretely enough that the section does not end abruptly; for an association, that means what to plan, measure, monitor, or test, not proof that a change in care would help. Work strengths into the points they support instead of giving them a separate paragraph, unless the venue asks for a strengths-and-limitations section. Close the interpretive part with a paragraph or sentence that ties the argument together, then give the limitations their own subsection. Where the journal has no Conclusions section, end with the take-home paragraph after the limitations. A Discussion does not owe every related study a paragraph; compare with the prior work the reader needs in order to judge the finding, in one place. When two paragraphs open alike or cover the same idea, merge them or restructure the section. When the Discussion reinterprets a result, cite its display, and treat main and supplementary displays the same way.

**Limitations.** Name the few limitations that could change the inference, each with its consequence for this study and, where the data allow, the evidence that bounds it, such as how much the estimate changed when the affected records were excluded. Keep them honest as well as brief, because readers need them to judge the inference. A bias known to act toward the null is handled where the result is interpreted, as [statistical reporting](statistical-reporting.md) describes. Leave out of the Limitations the analytic dependencies a reasonable reader would not question; the method itself is still described in the Methods, and the details stay available for reviewers.

**Abstract.** Name the design and the principal analytic approach in plain words where a reader would otherwise misjudge the strength of the evidence; a method's formal name earns abstract space only if the audience will recognize it. Give the primary outcome first; within it, an interpretable absolute measure can lead. Keep robustness summaries, such as medians across alternative specifications or counts of sensitivity analyses, out of the abstract. End on the take-home message, a sentence that ties the findings together and says what they mean for the reader. The design label and the design-matched verb carry the strength of the evidence. Where the venue format or reporting guideline has a limitations item, fill it with the limitation that most bounds the conclusion, in a clause. Otherwise a limitation sentence earns abstract space only when the conclusion would mislead without it; a generic call for validation or more research adds nothing. A rule or model evaluated only in the patients it was derived from is described as such where its performance is reported. The same holds for the conclusion.

**Introduction.** Three or four paragraphs usually suffice: why the problem matters to this reader, what current practice or evidence misses and why that matters now, why existing studies could not answer the question and what data or design can, and what this study did, ending with its hypothesis or question. State the hypothesis so that its answer is not obvious. “Patients referred during low staffing miss more visits” invites “of course”; “among patients with the same travel time, those referred during low staffing miss more visits” names the comparison that makes the question open. Sharpen the hypothesis the study set out to test; never substitute another, and state an objective when no hypothesis preceded the analysis. When citing prior work that asked a similar question, say in the same or the next sentence what it left open, from what the cited papers report; background that reports the same kind of result without its gap makes the paper look redundant. When earlier work already answered the question, present the study as a replication or an extension. The Introduction can pose the question in plain terms (“whether evening hours prevent the decline in attendance”); the design-matched wording governs the Results, Discussion, and Conclusion. Claims inside a hypothesis need citations like any other claim.

**Methods and Results.** Order the Methods so that each step has its context: the data and population before the unit of analysis, the outcomes before the models that use them. Label analyses by role (primary, secondary, sensitivity); see [statistical reporting](statistical-reporting.md) for planning status. In the Results, report every level of a graded comparison with its interval and say what each P value tests.

**Title.** Check recent titles of comparable studies in the target journal and follow their conventions for length, form and whether findings are stated. Weigh precedent, how easily the paper will be found and cited, and whether the title covers everything the paper actually examines. Empty labels such as "data-driven" can go.

For literature comparisons check population, comparator, time horizon, ascertainment, effect measure, adjustment, and evidence level before stating superiority or inconsistency. Different denominators or designs may explain an apparent contradiction. Label proposed mechanisms and inference explicitly.

For displays use [publication surfaces](publication-surface-standard.md). For reviewer responses use [revision and submission](revision-response-and-submission.md). A response's quoted revision and locator must match the final manuscript.
