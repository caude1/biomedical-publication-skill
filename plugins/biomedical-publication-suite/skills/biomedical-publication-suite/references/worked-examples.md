# From study evidence to publication prose

All source facts here are synthetic and belong only to their example. The evidence beside each edit is the basis for its specificity. Apply the reasoning to the available study output.

## Clinical motivation and title

**Source.** A referral system records whether a visit was booked within 14 days and whether the patient attended that visit. It records the first contact channel. Staff chose the channel.

**Weak framing.** “A comparison of two innovative engagement modalities.”

**Revision.** “Booking and attendance after outpatient rehabilitation referral.” The Introduction can ask whether differences in completed visits arise at scheduling or after a visit has been booked. The word *engagement* obscures two events that the service can measure separately. The records support comparing groups classified by initial contact. They provide no randomized comparison of invitation strategies.

## Work-log notes into Results

**Source.** First referrals for 350 adults. Of 200 initially contacted by portal, 100 booked within 14 days and 80 attended that visit. Of 150 initially contacted by telephone, 90 booked and 63 attended. Follow-up for these outcomes was complete.

**Draft.** “After correcting the script, the association reversed when we used the other denominator.”

**Revision.** “Among adults with a visit booked within 14 days, 80 of 100 (80%) in the portal group and 63 of 90 (70%) in the telephone group attended. Among all referred adults, 80 of 200 (40%) in the portal group and 63 of 150 (42%) in the telephone group attended such a visit.”

The two sentences answer different questions. Shortening the passage to “attendance was higher” leaves the reader to choose a denominator. The 14-day window applies to booking. Attendance can occur later.

## Abstract conclusion and Discussion

**Source.** In a separate cohort, low versus high clinic staffing was associated with a missed follow-up visit (adjusted OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.80). In the same adults, adding travel time to the model yielded an OR of 1.35 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.73). There was no mediation analysis.

**Abstract conclusion.** “Adults referred during periods of low clinic staffing had higher adjusted odds of missing a follow-up visit than those referred during periods of high staffing.”

**Discussion.** “The staffing association persisted after adjustment for travel time (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.73). The records identify when missed visits were more frequent, but do not distinguish difficulties scheduling an appointment from difficulties reaching the clinic.”

Changes in conditional odds ratios across models do not quantify a proportion mediated or localize where events arose. Naming the plausible next measurement gives the result a clinical implication without attributing the difference to an unmeasured mechanism.

## A subgroup or comparison outcome

**Source.** The model reported 30-day risks of an unplanned return and their ratios. The risk ratio was 0.82 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.96) on weekdays and 0.88 (95% CI 0.65 to 1.19) on weekends. No between-group contrast was estimated.

**Revision.** “The estimated risk ratio for an unplanned return was 0.82 on weekdays (95% CI 0.70 to 0.96) and 0.88 on weekends (95% CI 0.65 to 1.19).” Read the output before expanding the abbreviation RR, which is used for both risk ratios and rate ratios.

Different interval widths do not establish a weekday-specific effect. A secondary outcome can test whether a pattern extends to another clinical setting. Calling it a negative control requires a defensible rationale for the absence of the causal relationship and for the relevant shared biases.

## A limitation with a concrete consequence

**Source.** Travel distance was calculated from residence to clinic. Starting locations on the day of the visit were unavailable.

**Revision.** “Distance from home to the clinic may differ from the distance a patient traveled on the day of the visit, particularly for those leaving from work.”

The study measured home-to-clinic distance correctly. Its use as a proxy for travel on that day is the limitation. Bias direction depends on how the error relates to attendance and the analysis. Neither attenuation nor exaggeration can be asserted from these facts.

## A figure that readers may encounter first

**Source.** The referral counts above. Each person appears in only one invitation group, and booked patients are a subset of all referrals.

**Ambiguous caption.** “Attendance by invitation channel.”

**Revision.** “Booking and attendance by initial invitation channel. Panel A shows cumulative booking through day 14. In panel B, the first row gives attendance among all referrals and the second row among adults who booked within 14 days. Squares indicate differences between channels and horizontal lines indicate 95% confidence intervals. Attendance was assessed at the booked visit.”

Example labels such as “80/200 (40%)” and “80/100 (80%)” show how the same attendees enter two clinically different comparisons. Let the extent of the ambiguity determine whether to use a panel heading, axis label, aligned count column or a caption.

## A nonsignificant result the author wants to clarify

**Source.** A superiority trial estimated a difference of −1.7 points (95% CI −4.3 to 0.9). Lower scores were better. An equivalence margin was never defined.

**Revision.** “The intervention group scored 1.7 points lower on average than the control group (mean difference −1.7, 95% CI −4.3 to 0.9).” If clinically important differences have been defined and verified, discuss what range remains compatible with the data. “The interventions performed equally well” would require additional evidence.

## A missing fact during a style edit

**Draft.** “Experts agree that delayed follow-up is a growing problem.” No supporting source or time series was supplied.

**Decision.** Remove the unsupported prevalence trend. In the referral study, start instead from the concrete question of booking and attendance. If the author specifically needs a time trend for their opening argument, locate a suitable source or identify that gap in an author note. A request for stronger prose supplies no new evidence.

## Register: three ways to miss, then the plain version

**Source.** The referral counts above.

**Performed.** “The portal's advantage is real, but it is an illusion of the denominator: booked is not attended.”

**Loose.** “Portal patients who actually got around to booking mostly showed up, but plenty never booked at all.”

**Convoluted.** “Attendance, higher after portal contact among those booking, though not among all referred, of whom a smaller share booked, did not differ overall.”

**Revision.** “Among adults who booked a visit within 14 days, 80% of those contacted by portal and 70% of those contacted by telephone attended. A smaller proportion of the portal group booked (50% vs 60%), so attendance among all referred adults was similar in the two groups (40% and 42%).”

The first version stages a reveal with a colon and closes on an aphorism. The second is conversational and contains no number. The third packs three comparisons into one sentence that has to be read twice. The revision gives each comparison its own clause, denominator and numbers, in sentences a journal would print. Correcting one fault often produces another, so rebuild the sentence from what it has to say.

## Limitations that bound the inference

**Source.** Staff chose the contact channel and the records do not say why. All referrals came from one health system in which most patients already had a portal account.

**Draft.** “This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design is subject to bias. Second, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, the findings may not be generalizable. Fourth, some data were missing. Despite these limitations, our findings provide important insights.”

**Revision.** “Two limitations bear on the comparison. Staff chose the contact channel, and the records do not show why. If staff telephoned the patients they expected to have difficulty booking online, the groups differed before contact in ways these data cannot describe. In addition, most patients in this health system already had a portal account, and the results may not apply where portal use is uncommon.”

Name the limitations that could change the inference, each with its consequence for this study. The generic threats, the numbered list and the closing pivot add nothing a reader can use. The draft's fourth item is also untrue here, because follow-up was complete. Smaller concerns can wait until a reviewer raises them.

## A conclusion that stands alone

**Draft.** “In conclusion, the contact channel matters. Further research is needed.”

**Revision.** “Among 350 adults referred for outpatient rehabilitation, 50% of those first contacted by portal and 60% of those contacted by telephone booked a visit within 14 days, whereas 80% and 70% of those who booked attended. As a result, a similar proportion of each group attended a visit (40% and 42%). Services comparing contact methods should measure booking and attendance separately, because a difference at one step can be offset at the next.”

Some readers read only the conclusion. It restates the population, the findings with their numbers and the implication the evidence supports, and it ends on that implication.

## Cover letter and reviewer response

**Cover letter, from the referral data.** “We submit ‘Booking and attendance after outpatient rehabilitation referral’ as a Brief Report. Attendance among patients who booked was higher after portal contact, yet a smaller proportion of portal-first referrals resulted in a booking. Following referrals from first contact to the visit gives outpatient services a way to identify where patients are lost to follow-up.” Use the venue's actual article type and explain the fit for its readership.

**Response, after the revised figure and legend were checked.** “We agree that the original legend left the denominator unclear. Figure 1 now shows attendance among all referrals and among patients with a booking, with counts for each channel. We also distinguish the two populations in the Results, paragraph 2.” Before making this claim in an actual response, reopen the delivered version and verify the figure and locator.
